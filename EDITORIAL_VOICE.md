# Brali editorial voice

Brali does not publish advice-shaped wallpaper. A useful entry should teach a reader enough to understand the move, try it, notice what happened, and know where the evidence stops.

## The voice

Write like a smart, practical person explaining something useful to another smart person.

- Clear before clever.
- Concrete before inspirational.
- Explain the mechanism in ordinary language.
- Prefer a small experiment over a life rule.
- Name uncertainty without turning the page into legal boilerplate.
- Use examples that make the action easy to imagine.
- Let the reader understand *why* a step exists, not just copy instructions.

The tone may be informal. Dry, restrained sarcasm is welcome when it is aimed at bad advice, fake precision, productivity theatre, pseudoscientific certainty, or magical numbers. It must never be aimed at the reader, a vulnerable group, symptoms, or scientific uncertainty.

Examples of acceptable Brali lines:

- "Five minutes is a container, not a prescription. Your timer did not graduate from medical school."
- "Remembering the rule and using the rule are two different jobs. Annoying, but useful to know."
- "If the system needs seventeen tags before you can save a note, congratulations: you have created a second job."

Avoid jokes when the topic is safety-sensitive, clinical, crisis-related, or likely to make a reader feel dismissed.

## What every strong protocol teaches

A strong page should usually make these ideas obvious without forcing the reader to decode the structure:

1. **The problem** — when this protocol is useful.
2. **The mechanism** — what variable the protocol is trying to change.
3. **The move** — the smallest concrete action worth trying.
4. **The signal** — what to observe instead of relying on vibes.
5. **The review point** — when to decide whether to keep, change, or stop.
6. **The boundary** — what the evidence does *not* establish.
7. **The alternative** — what to try when this approach does not fit.

Brali should leave the reader slightly more capable after reading the page, even if they never use the protocol.

## Evidence without theatre

Do not decorate a weak recommendation with scientific vocabulary.

Avoid:

- invented optimal durations, counts, percentages, streaks, or thresholds;
- "dopamine", "cortisol", "neuroplasticity", "rewiring", or other neuroscience language unless the mechanism is actually necessary and directly supported by the reviewed source;
- turning an association into a causal instruction;
- turning one RCT into a universal rule;
- turning a meta-analysis average into a guaranteed individual effect;
- implying that a recorded citation automatically makes the page evidence-based.

A useful evidence paragraph should answer: **What did the research actually test, in whom, against what, and what should we *not* conclude from it?**

## Explain negative findings too

Null and mixed results are product information, not editorial inconveniences. They often make a protocol better.

If research shows that a technique helps idea generation but not idea selection, split the workflow. If it improves recall but not application, teach both stages. If a promising intervention is difficult to adhere to, reduce the first step rather than hiding the adherence problem.

"It did not work for everything" is often exactly the boundary that makes a Brali protocol credible.

## Old content is allowed to get better

The migrated library is raw material, not scripture. Reviewed and practical entries may be rewritten when the new version:

- is easier to understand;
- removes generic or AI-sounding prose;
- explains the mechanism more clearly;
- removes unsupported precision;
- adds a useful evidence boundary;
- improves examples, first steps, review signals, or alternatives;
- preserves provenance and does not silently upgrade the evidence state.

Prefer improving a strong existing entry over creating a near-duplicate just to increase the library count.

## Search and AI discovery

Write for humans first, but make the meaning easy for search systems and AI agents to retrieve.

Use descriptive headings, explicit problem language, concrete actions, observable outcomes, source titles, and bounded claims. Do not pad pages with SEO filler, keyword variants, or generic FAQs that teach nothing.

The goal is not to look authoritative. The goal is to be useful enough that authority can be checked.
