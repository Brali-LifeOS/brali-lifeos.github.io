Use Your Senses to Return to the Present

Practice Daily Mindfulness

Protocol summary · reviewed

Try this

Pause somewhere safe and deliberately notice what is happening around you right now. Choose a few sights, sounds, textures, smells or tastes and describe them simply. When your attention drifts into planning, worry or commentary, notice that and gently return to one sensory detail.

Check-in: What did I notice, how easy or difficult was it to return attention to the present, and did this feel useful enough to try again?

Evidence: Reviewed · source · NHS Every Mind Matters — What is mindfulness?

Review note: Public content is replaced by a sourced present-moment sensory mindfulness protocol grounded in current NHS guidance. Fixed three-to-five-minute dosing, weekly targets, invented adherence observations and treatment-like expectations are removed.

Safety: This is general educational material, not medical advice, diagnosis, treatment, or a substitute for professional care.

Mindfulness is about paying attention to what is happening in the present moment. NHS guidance suggests noticing the world around you, including shapes, colours, textures, sounds, smells and other sensations. Brali turns that into a small observation experiment. The goal is not to empty your mind or guarantee calm. It is simply to practise noticing what is here and returning attention when it drifts.

1. Choose a safe pause

Use the exercise when you can safely give some attention to your surroundings. Do not do it while driving, crossing a road, operating machinery or doing another task that requires your full attention.

2. Notice a few sensory details

Look around and choose a few concrete details. You might notice the colour and shape of an object, a sound in the room, the texture of clothing or a chair, a smell, or a taste. You do not need to use every sense or follow a fixed count.

3. Describe what is present

Use simple descriptions such as warm, rough, quiet, bright or distant. The point is to contact the experience that is happening now rather than turning the exercise into analysis or a test of whether you are becoming calm enough.

4. Notice when attention wanders

If you start planning, worrying or judging the exercise, simply notice that attention moved. NHS mindfulness guidance also suggests stepping back from thoughts and naming them. You can acknowledge the thought and return to one sensory detail.

5. Stop when the pause is enough

There is no required three-minute, five-minute or weekly target. A short pause can be enough for the experiment. If continuing feels unhelpful or uncomfortable, stop and return to what you were doing.

6. Review your own signal

In Brali, record what you noticed and whether returning attention to the present felt useful, neutral or difficult. Do not expect a prescribed reduction in stress or anxiety. If significant anxiety, fear or other mental-health difficulties are affecting daily life, use appropriate professional support rather than relying on this exercise alone.

Questions to consider

Does the practice need to last 3 to 5 minutes?

No. Three minutes can be a convenient boundary, but Brali does not treat it as an evidence-based dose. NHS mindfulness guidance includes very brief present-moment exercises such as taking a minute to notice the room around you.

What if my mind keeps wandering?

That does not make the practice a failure. Notice where attention went and gently return to something you can see, hear, feel, smell or taste. The exercise is about returning attention to the present, not eliminating thought.

Is this a treatment for anxiety or another mental-health condition?

No. Brali presents this as a general mindfulness exercise. NHS guidance notes that mindfulness may support mental wellbeing, but if anxiety, fear or another difficulty is affecting daily life or self-help is not enough, appropriate professional support is available.
