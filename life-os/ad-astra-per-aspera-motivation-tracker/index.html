Use a Short Phrase to Choose the Next Step

Ad Astra Per Aspera (To the Stars Through Difficulties)

Protocol summary · practical

Try this

When you feel stuck, pause and repeat a short phrase once. Name what is difficult in plain language, then choose one action that is small enough to begin now. Skip the phrase if it feels hollow, pressuring or unrelated to the real problem.

Check-in: What was difficult, what action did I choose, and did the phrase help me begin or should I use a different cue next time?

A short phrase can act as a transition between feeling stuck and choosing what to do next. It does not make hardship meaningful and it does not solve the underlying problem. Use it only when it helps you face the situation clearly and begin one controllable action.

1. Choose a grounded phrase

Use Ad astra per aspera, one step at a time, or another line that feels credible to you. Avoid language that demands constant toughness or treats suffering as a requirement for success.

2. Name what is difficult

Describe the immediate problem without turning it into an identity statement: I do not know how to start this draft, I need to ask for help, or this plan no longer fits.

3. Choose one controllable action

Pick the smallest action that changes the situation: open the document, write the question, move the deadline, contact the person, take a real break, or decide that continuing is not the right move.

4. Review whether the cue helped

After the action, note whether the phrase helped you begin, made no difference, or added pressure. Keep it only if it supports clearer action.

5. Do not use a motto to override a boundary

A phrase should not persuade you to remain in an unsafe, harmful or pointless situation. Change the plan, seek support or stop when that is the more responsible action.

Questions to consider

Do I need to use the Latin phrase?

No. Use plain language if it feels more honest. A useful cue should help you notice the situation and choose an action, not impress you with its wording.

What if the phrase feels dismissive?

Drop it. Some difficulties need rest, support, a boundary, a changed plan or professional help rather than a motivational line.

What should I track?

Record the situation, the next action you chose and whether you started it. The useful signal is what happened after the cue, not how many times you repeated it.

Try it, then review.

Use the action above as a starting point. Adapt it to your situation, notice what happens, and keep, change, or drop it.
