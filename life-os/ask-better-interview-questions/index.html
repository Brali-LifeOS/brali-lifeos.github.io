Run a Short, Concrete Information Interview

Conduct Interviews

Protocol summary · practical

Try this

Explain why you are asking and get permission. Ask the person to walk through the most recent relevant example. Use one question at a time about sequence, context, tools, decisions and uncertainty. Summarize what you heard and invite correction.

Check-in: What concrete sequence did I learn, what remains an estimate or interpretation, and what did the person correct?

Review note: The inherited interview guide contained unsupported multipliers, sample claims and fabricated observations. The effective public record is rewritten as a consent-based, sequential information interview without an evidence claim.

A useful interview produces a concrete, correctable account rather than an impressive pile of questions. Start with one recent event, move through it in sequence and check that your notes match what the person meant.

1. Set the purpose and ask permission

State what you are trying to understand and how the information will be used. Ask whether the person is comfortable discussing the example and respect any boundary they set.

2. Choose one recent example

Ask about the last relevant event rather than the person's general theory of what usually happens. A specific example gives both of you something concrete to inspect.

3. Follow the sequence one step at a time

Begin with what happened first? and continue with the next step. Ask separately about context, tools, handoffs, decisions and workarounds instead of combining several questions.

4. Label facts, estimates and interpretations

When the person is unsure, record the answer as an estimate. Keep your inference separate from what they directly described, especially when interpreting motives or causes.

5. Summarize and invite correction

Repeat the sequence and the important distinction in your own words. Ask what you missed or misunderstood before using the notes to make a decision.

Questions to consider

How do I keep the conversation from feeling like an interrogation?

Explain the purpose, ask permission, keep the scope narrow and let the person skip a question. Curiosity does not cancel consent.

What should I do with a vague answer?

Ask for the most recent example and one step at a time. Accept uncertainty instead of forcing a precise number or explanation the person cannot recall.

How do I avoid misquoting the person?

Summarize the sequence and key distinction back to them, then invite correction. Keep facts, estimates and your interpretation labelled separately.

Try it, then review.

Use the action above as a starting point. Adapt it to your situation, notice what happens, and keep, change, or drop it.
