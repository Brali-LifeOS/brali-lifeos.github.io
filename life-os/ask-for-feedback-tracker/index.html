Ask for Feedback on One Specific Decision

Double-Check Your Knowledge (Dunning-Kruger Effect)

Protocol summary · practical

Try this

State the intended outcome and the current decision. Ask one focused question such as what is unclear, what assumption looks wrong or what should change first. Choose a reviewer who can judge that question, then record what you will accept, test, discuss, defer or ignore.

Check-in: What did I ask, what did the reviewer actually say, and what decision or test changed because of it?

Evidence: Practical guidance; no evidence-like claim is detected in the current source record.

Review note: The inherited feedback article contained unsupported internal trials, cohort outcomes, response-rate claims and arbitrary quotas. The effective public record is rewritten as a bounded feedback request and decision protocol without an evidence claim.

Feedback is easier to use when the reviewer knows the intended outcome and the decision you are making. Ask one question that fits their experience, then turn the response into an explicit decision rather than collecting comments indefinitely.

1. Choose one decision or artifact

Use a draft, slide, design, message, plan or piece of work that can still change. State the intended audience and outcome so the reviewer knows what standard to apply.

2. Ask one focused question

Replace any thoughts? with a question such as what is unclear?, which assumption would you challenge?, or what should change before this is ready?

3. Choose a relevant reviewer

Ask someone who understands the audience, domain, decision or implementation constraint. A senior title does not automatically make feedback relevant.

4. Separate the signal from the delivery

Pause before editing. Identify the concrete observation, the reviewer's interpretation and any proposed change. A harsh tone can contain a useful signal, and a kind tone can still contain a weak suggestion.

5. Record what changes

Choose an explicit disposition: accept, test, discuss, defer or reject. Note the reason and the next action so the feedback loop ends in a decision rather than a growing comment archive.

Questions to consider

What if reviewers disagree?

Compare each comment with the intended outcome and ask for the reasoning behind the difference. You may need a small test or a decision from the accountable owner rather than a vote.

Do I need to accept every comment?

No. Feedback is information, not an instruction. Record why you accepted, tested, discussed, deferred or rejected it.

How often should I ask?

Ask at useful decision points, when the cost of being wrong matters or when another perspective can reveal a blind spot. Avoid a quota that creates requests with no clear purpose.

Try it, then review.

Use the action above as a starting point. Adapt it to your situation, notice what happens, and keep, change, or drop it.
