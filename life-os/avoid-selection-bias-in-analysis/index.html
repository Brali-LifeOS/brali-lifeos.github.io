Write the Inclusion Rule Before Generalizing

Spot Conditional Pitfalls

Protocol summary · practical

Try this

State the question and population you want to understand. Record how cases entered the analysis, which cases were excluded or missing and whether selection depends on the outcome. Compare the conclusion with a broader view or label it as applying only to the selected group. Write what additional data would change the conclusion.

Check-in: Who was included, who was missing, and is my conclusion limited to the selected group or supported beyond it?

Review note: The inherited article contained unsupported mistake reduction, conversion examples, sample prescriptions and statistical precision. The effective public record is rewritten as an inclusion-rule and conclusion-scope check without an evidence claim.

A clean result can describe only the cases that were allowed into the analysis. Before turning it into a general rule, inspect the selection process and keep the conclusion inside the group the data can represent.

1. Define the target group

Write who, what or which events the decision is meant to cover. This is the population your conclusion would need to represent.

2. Write the inclusion rule

Record how cases entered the report: threshold, response, purchase, survival, availability, completion, manual choice or another filter.

3. Name excluded and missing cases

List who could not enter, did not respond, dropped out or was removed. Note whether the reason for absence is related to the outcome being discussed.

4. Test the scope of the conclusion

Compare with a broader group when data is available. Otherwise rewrite the conclusion so it applies only to the selected cases and state the missing information.

5. Record what would change the conclusion

Identify the missing group, alternative sample or additional comparison needed before a broader decision is justified. Keep that need attached to the result.

Questions to consider

Does a selected sample make the analysis useless?

No. It may answer a narrower question. The problem begins when a result for the selected group is presented as though it applies to cases that were not represented.

What if excluded cases cannot be measured?

Describe the missing group, explain why it is missing and narrow the conclusion. Record what information would be needed before making a broader claim.

Is checking the inclusion rule enough to remove bias?

No. It is a diagnostic step. Other problems can remain in measurement, comparison, timing, missing data or interpretation.

Try it, then review.

Use the action above as a starting point. Adapt it to your situation, notice what happens, and keep, change, or drop it.
