Action and Mood Observation Note

Record what you did and what you noticed afterward

Protocol summary · reviewed

Try this

Write the situation, the activity you chose, how you felt before, and how you felt afterward. Add one alternative explanation so the note does not turn correlation into certainty.

Check-in: What did you observe, and what would be worth trying or discussing next without assuming the activity caused the change?

Evidence: Reviewed · source · NHS describes CBT as working with difficult situations and how people think, feel and act, often using practice between sessions and worksheets or diaries.

Review note: Reviewed against NHS CBT and thought-record guidance. Public copy is an educational observation worksheet about situation, action and before/after experience; it is not presented as formal CBT, diagnosis, treatment, or proof of causality.

Safety: This is general educational material, not medical advice, diagnosis, treatment, or a substitute for professional care.

A short activity note can make a vague impression more inspectable. The purpose is to record what happened and compare observations, not to diagnose yourself or prove that one behavior caused a particular feeling.

1. Name the situation

Write a short factual description of what was happening before the activity. Keep interpretation separate from the event where possible.

2. Record the before state

Use a few words to describe how you felt or what you were thinking before the activity. A numeric rating is optional, not required.

3. Record the action and after state

Write what you actually did and what you noticed afterward. Do not rewrite the plan as if it happened.

4. Add an alternative explanation

Ask what else might have influenced the change. This keeps the note useful as an observation instead of turning it into an unsupported causal claim.

Questions to consider

Is this the same as receiving CBT?

No. It is a simple educational observation note inspired by common CBT-style recording practices. Formal CBT involves a structured therapeutic process and may involve a trained therapist.

Does feeling better after an activity prove the activity caused it?

No. Many things can change at the same time. Record the observation and, when useful, test the pattern again rather than treating one entry as proof.

What if writing about the situation makes distress worse?

Stop the exercise. A self-guided note is optional and should not replace professional support when distress is significant or persistent.
