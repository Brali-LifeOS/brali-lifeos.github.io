Draw Two Circles; One for Factors You Can Control and Another for Those You

Focus on What You Can Do

Protocol summary · practical

Try this

Write down one concern, separate the outcome from the actions you can take, convert anything you can influence into one concrete behavior, and choose the next useful action without pretending you control the final result.

Check-in: What part of this situation was actually mine to act on, what remained outside my control, and did the sorting help me choose a clearer next step?

A control-circle review is a way to separate outcomes from actions. Start with one concern, identify what you can actually do, translate influence into a concrete behavior, and leave the final outcome separate. The purpose is not emotional detachment. It is to make the next useful action easier to see.

1. Write the concern as it appears

Start with the thing occupying attention: The client may reject the proposal, The weather may disrupt the trip, or I do not know how the team will react. Do not solve it yet.

2. Separate the outcome from your actions

Put outcomes that depend on other people, chance, timing, or external conditions outside your control. Put your own observable behaviors on the control side.

Outcome: The client approves the proposal.
Actions: Clarify the summary, check the numbers, ask one colleague to review it, and send it by the agreed time.

3. Convert influence into behavior

Many situations are not simply controllable or uncontrollable. You can influence them. Instead of creating a permanent gray category, ask: What can I actually do that may improve the odds or prepare me for the result?

Write that behavior on the control side and keep the uncertain outcome separate.

4. Choose one next action

Pick the smallest useful action from the control side. Make it concrete enough to start: draft three bullets, send one question, review the first page, book the appointment, or prepare the fallback option.

If there is no useful action now, decide whether the item needs monitoring later or simply remains uncertain.

5. Review whether the sorting changed behavior

After acting, ask:

  • Did I spend effort on something I could actually change?
  • Did I turn an uncertain outcome into an action by mistake?
  • Is there another useful action, or is the next step to wait, monitor, or accept uncertainty?

The exercise is useful when it changes allocation of attention or action. It does not require you to stop caring about the outcome.

6. Keep the action, not the diagram

In Brali, the lasting record can be simple: the concern, the controllable action, and a short review of what happened. The circles are only a thinking aid. Once the next action is clear, the action matters more than maintaining a perfect diagram.

Questions to consider

What belongs in the control side?

Your own observable choices and actions: what you say, prepare, send, practice, ask, schedule, or stop doing. An outcome that depends on other people, chance, or external conditions does not become controllable just because you care about it.

What about things I can influence but not control?

Translate influence into an action you can take. For example, you cannot control whether a proposal is approved, but you can clarify the argument, ask for feedback, or prepare evidence. Keep the outcome separate from the behavior.

Does putting something outside my control mean I should ignore it?

No. It means you stop describing the outcome as something you can guarantee. You may still monitor it, prepare for it, ask for help, or decide how you will respond if it changes.

Try it, then review.

Use the action above as a starting point. Adapt it to your situation, notice what happens, and keep, change, or drop it.
