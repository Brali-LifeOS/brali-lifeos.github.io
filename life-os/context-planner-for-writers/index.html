Write a Context Brief Before Drafting

Name the reader, publication surface, purpose and constraints before choosing structure and tone.

Protocol summary · practical

Try this

Write the primary reader, publication surface, desired reader action or understanding, required tone or format constraints, facts that must be checked, and any detail that should be omitted for privacy, confidentiality or scope. Then build the outline from that brief.

Check-in: Who is this for, where will it appear, what should the reader understand or do, and which constraint or fact must the draft respect?

Writing decisions become easier when the context is explicit before the first paragraph. A short brief defines the reader, publication surface, purpose, constraints and verification needs. Those choices become the filter for structure, tone and supporting detail.

1. Name the primary reader

Describe the reader by the task they are trying to complete or the decision they need to make. Avoid invented demographic detail unless it is genuinely relevant and known.

2. Record the publication surface

Note where the text will appear and the actual constraints of that surface: required format, available space, link behavior, confidentiality, moderation rules, accessibility needs or organizational standards.

3. Define the intended outcome

State what the reader should understand, decide or do after reading. When the text has several goals, identify which one controls the main path and which can remain secondary.

4. Set verification and content boundaries

List the facts that need checking and the information that should not be included. Use the brief to choose the outline and tone, then revisit it when the draft starts serving a different audience or purpose.

Questions to consider

What if the text has several audiences?

Choose the reader who drives the main path and record secondary audiences separately. If their needs conflict materially, create separate versions or clearly separated layers rather than forcing every purpose into one paragraph.

Should the brief specify a word count?

Only when the publication surface or task has a real length constraint. Otherwise define the information the reader needs and remove material that does not serve the purpose rather than inventing an arbitrary target.

What should I verify before drafting?

Identify claims, names, dates, figures, quotations, permissions, links and other details whose accuracy or provenance matters. Mark unknowns in the brief so they are not silently filled with plausible wording during drafting.
