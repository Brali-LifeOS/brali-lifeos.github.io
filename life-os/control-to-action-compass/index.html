Separate Controllable Actions From Dependencies and Outcomes

Turn a concern into the next action you can actually perform.

Protocol summary · practical

Try this

Write the concern or desired outcome, list the actions you can directly perform, list dependencies or responses owned by others, identify outcomes you can only observe, choose one next action under your control, and give waiting items a review trigger rather than repeatedly treating them as current work.

Check-in: What action can I perform directly, what depends on someone or something else, what outcome can I only observe, and when will I revisit the waiting part?

Review note: The inherited article contained therapy and physiological framing, family-health examples, fabricated completion effects, fixed concern counts and timing rules. The effective public record is narrowed to separating directly controllable actions from dependencies and outcomes, then scheduling one concrete next action without claiming emotional or clinical effects.

Many concerns contain several different things at once: an action you can perform, a response owned by someone else, and an outcome no one can directly command. Separating those parts turns a vague concern into a plan without pretending that the plan controls the result.

1. State the concern or outcome

Write the situation in one sentence. Then identify the result you care about without turning that result itself into a task. A task should describe something an owner can actually do.

2. Separate actions, dependencies and outcomes

Put directly performable actions in one group. Put approvals, replies, deliveries and other external dependencies in another. Put final outcomes that can only be observed in a third group.

3. Choose one controllable next action

Select the action that moves the situation or reduces uncertainty now: draft, ask, schedule, prepare, send, check, decide, document or escalate. Give it a real owner and place in the work system.

4. Give dependencies a review trigger

Move external dependencies out of the active action list once your part is complete. Record what you are waiting for and the condition that should bring the item back into attention.

Questions to consider

Is influence the same as control?

No. You may control the action of asking, proposing, preparing or following up, while the other person's response remains outside your control. Keep those two parts separate so the plan does not depend on pretending the response is guaranteed.

What should I do with something I cannot act on yet?

Give it a waiting state with a trigger such as a date, reply, delivery, approval or other event. Revisit it when the trigger occurs rather than repeatedly carrying the same item as an active task.

Does this mean ignoring outcomes I cannot control?

No. Outcomes and dependencies still matter for planning and risk. The distinction only clarifies where direct action is possible and where monitoring, contingency planning, escalation or waiting is the appropriate next state.
