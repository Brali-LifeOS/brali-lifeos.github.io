Shared-Value Option Map

Look for trades that improve both sides

Protocol summary · practical

Try this

List what each side wants, what each side can trade at relatively low cost, and two or three options that combine those differences. Then test the options with the other side.

Check-in: Did the conversation uncover a real trade, or are the interests still incompatible enough that a clear boundary or no-deal option is better?

Evidence: Practical guidance; no evidence-like claim is detected in the current source record.

Review note: Public content is replaced by an interest-and-trade option map. It does not promise mutual gain or imply that every conflict has a win-win solution; a clear no-deal or boundary remains valid.

Many negotiations get stuck because each side argues for a position before understanding what the other side actually values. An option map delays that argument long enough to look for useful trades.

1. List the interests

Write what matters to each side in plain language: speed, certainty, cost, flexibility, recognition, quality, risk, or another real constraint.

2. Find differences in value

Look for something that is relatively cheap for one side and valuable to the other. Those differences create possible trades.

3. Build more than one option

Create several combinations before choosing. More than one option makes it easier to compare trade-offs instead of defending the first idea.

4. Keep a no-deal option

Do not force the language of mutual gain when the fit is poor. A clear boundary or respectful no-deal can be better than a fragile agreement.

Questions to consider

Does every disagreement have a win-win solution?

No. Some interests genuinely conflict. The map is useful because it reveals possible trades and also makes incompatibility easier to see.

What should I list first?

Start with interests and constraints, not demands. Two people can reject each other's positions while still having room to trade on timing, scope, effort, risk, or format.
