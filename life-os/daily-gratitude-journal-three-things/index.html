Specific Gratitude Note

Notice one to three concrete things without forcing positivity

Protocol summary · reviewed

Try this

Choose one to three concrete moments, people, resources or actions from the day. Write what happened and one sentence about why it mattered to you. Skip the practice when it feels forced or unhelpful.

Check-in: Did the note help you notice something specific, or did it feel mechanical, invalidating, or like another obligation? Keep, change, or drop it accordingly.

Evidence: Reviewed · source · A 2025 preregistered meta-analysis across 145 papers found a small overall increase in well-being from gratitude interventions, with meaningful cross-country variability; other systematic reviews find several physical-health outcomes mixed or under-studied.

Review note: Reviewed against recent gratitude meta-analysis and systematic-review evidence. Public copy preserves the small/variable-effect boundary, removes fixed dosage and guaranteed mood claims, and says not to force gratitude or substitute it for mental-health care.

Safety: This is general educational material, not medical advice, diagnosis, treatment, or a substitute for professional care.

Gratitude exercises have been studied widely, but the useful boundary matters: average effects are modest, outcomes vary, and a gratitude list is not a treatment plan. Brali therefore keeps the practice small, optional and specific.

1. Choose something specific

Pick one to three concrete moments, people, resources or actions rather than a generic category such as “everything” or “life.”

2. Say why it mattered

Add one short sentence about why the item mattered to you. Description is enough; there is no requirement to produce a strong positive emotion.

3. Do not force positivity

Gratitude should not be used to erase grief, anger, fear or legitimate problems. Skip the exercise when it feels invalidating or mechanical.

4. Review whether it is useful

After several uses, ask whether the note changes what you notice or value. If it simply creates another obligation, reduce the frequency or stop.

Questions to consider

Do I need to write exactly three things every day?

No. One specific item can be enough. The evidence does not justify treating three items or a daily schedule as a universal optimum.

Should this make me feel better immediately?

Not necessarily. Effects reported across studies are generally modest and variable. Brali treats the note as an optional reflection practice, not a guaranteed mood intervention.

What if gratitude feels wrong during grief or severe distress?

Do not force it. You can skip the practice entirely. It is not a substitute for appropriate support or mental-health care.
