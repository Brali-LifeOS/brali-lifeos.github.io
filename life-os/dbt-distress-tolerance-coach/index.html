Pause Before Acting on a High-Stress Urge

A bounded DBT-informed distress-tolerance pause, not crisis treatment

Protocol summary · reviewed

Try this

Pause. Describe the situation in one factual sentence and name the action urge without judging it. Choose a harmless brief activity that creates time rather than escalating the situation. Then reassess what action is safest and most useful.

Check-in: Did you create a pause without making the situation worse, and what safe next action did you choose? Do not score success by whether the emotion disappeared.

Evidence: Reviewed · source · Dorset HealthCare describes DBT distress tolerance as learning to tolerate emotional pain in difficult situations rather than immediately changing it, alongside mindfulness and other skills. A systematic review found preliminary support for stand-alone DBT skills training but concluded that methodological limitations prevent strong efficacy conclusions.

Review note: Reviewed against Dorset HealthCare NHS descriptions of DBT skills and the Valentine et al. systematic review of DBT skills training used without other DBT modalities. Public content is narrowed to a low-risk observe-pause-reassess exercise, removes cold-exposure/physiological instructions, and explicitly states that evidence does not make it equivalent to full DBT or crisis treatment.

Safety: This is general educational material, not medical advice, diagnosis, treatment, or a substitute for professional care.

DBT includes mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation and interpersonal-effectiveness skills. This Brali page uses only a narrow idea from the first two: notice what is happening, create a safe pause, and avoid making a difficult moment worse. It is not a compressed version of DBT treatment.

1. Check that a pause is appropriate

Use this only when the situation is safe enough to stop briefly. If there is immediate danger, a medical emergency, or risk of harm, move to appropriate real-world support instead of this exercise.

2. Observe and describe

State the situation in one factual sentence. Then name the urge you notice, such as leaving, sending a message, arguing, buying something, or avoiding the task. An urge is information, not an instruction.

3. Create a harmless short delay

Choose a brief action that does not escalate the problem: sit somewhere safe, drink ordinary water, walk to another room, write the unsent message in notes, or do another neutral activity. The purpose is simply to create time before acting.

4. Reassess the next action

After the pause, ask what action is safest, useful and consistent with your responsibilities. The emotion does not have to disappear for the pause to have done its job.

Evidence and care boundary

DBT skills are normally taught within a broader structured treatment. Research on skills training used alone is promising in some settings but does not justify treating this simplified exercise as equivalent to full DBT or as a treatment for a mental-health condition.

Questions to consider

Is this the same as DBT treatment?

No. Full DBT is a structured treatment with multiple components. This page presents one small educational exercise inspired by distress-tolerance and mindfulness skills, not a replacement for DBT.

Should I use this during a crisis or when I may harm myself or someone else?

No. This protocol assumes the situation is safe enough for an ordinary pause. It is not crisis planning or emergency support. Use appropriate professional or emergency support when safety is at risk.

Why does this version not use ice water or extreme cold?

Brali deliberately keeps this public starter low risk. It does not prescribe cold exposure, breath holding, or other physiological maneuvers that can require additional safety screening and context.
