Review a Decision Using What Was Known at the Time

Separate the quality of the process from the luck of the outcome.

Protocol summary · practical

Try this

Write the decision and its date, list the information and constraints that were available then, separate later discoveries into a different section, note which realistic alternatives were considered or missed, identify one process weakness or strength, and define one change to use for a similar future decision.

Check-in: What was genuinely knowable at the decision point, what did I learn only afterward, and what should change in the process next time?

Evidence: Practical guidance; no evidence-like claim is detected in the current source record.

Review note: The inherited article mixed unsupported decision-quality effects with invented timing and memory thresholds, fabricated first-party results, arbitrary scores, and emotional-regulation instructions. The effective public record is narrowed to reconstructing what was actually known at the decision point, separating later information, inspecting concrete process strengths and omissions, and carrying one process improvement into the next similar decision.

A result can make an earlier choice look obviously good or obviously bad. A useful retrospective separates the outcome from the information that existed when the choice was made. That creates a fairer process record and, more importantly, a concrete improvement for the next similar decision.

1. Reconstruct the decision point

Write what was being decided, who owned the decision, the date or stage, and the constraints that were active. Then collect the artifacts that existed at that point. Do not start by explaining the later outcome.

2. Separate then-known facts from later information

Create two lists: information available before the decision and information learned afterward. If an item is based only on memory, mark that uncertainty. This prevents later knowledge from quietly changing what the original decision environment looked like.

3. Inspect the process, not just the result

Ask whether important constraints were visible, alternatives were considered, dissent or uncertainty was recorded, ownership was clear, and a fallback existed where one was needed. Name specific omissions instead of assigning a vague good-or-bad score.

4. Carry one improvement forward

Choose one process change that would improve a similar future decision: capture a missing input, require a named owner, document an assumption, add a rollback trigger, or request a second view. Put that change where the next decision will actually encounter it.

Questions to consider

Does a good process make a bad outcome acceptable?

No. Outcomes still matter. The process review asks a different question: whether the decision used the information, constraints, alternatives, and responsibilities available at the time, and what should be improved before a similar choice recurs.

What if I cannot remember what was known then?

Prefer contemporaneous artifacts such as tickets, notes, messages, dashboards, approvals, or decision records. Mark uncertain memories as uncertain rather than turning hindsight into a reconstructed fact.

Try it, then review.

Use the action above as a starting point. Adapt it to your situation, notice what happens, and keep, change, or drop it.
