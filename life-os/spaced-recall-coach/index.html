Separate Review Sessions Instead of Cramming

Space repeated review around how long you need to remember the material

Protocol summary · reviewed

Try this

Choose one set of verbal material and decide how long you need to retain it. Split the planned repetitions across at least two sessions separated by time. Use a longer gap when the required retention period is longer, then check final recall and adjust the next schedule instead of treating one interval as universally optimal.

Check-in: Did I separate the study episodes rather than cram them together, and did the chosen gap fit the period over which I needed to remember the material?

Evidence: Reviewed · source · Cepeda NJ, Pashler H, Vul E, Wixted JT, Rohrer D. Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: a review and quantitative synthesis. Psychological Bulletin. 2006;132(3):354-380.

Review note: The effective public protocol was fully rewritten to match the reviewed distributed-practice boundary: separated study sessions for verbal material, spacing chosen in relation to the intended retention period, explicit uncertainty about exact intervals, and no claim of transfer beyond verbal recall.

Repeated study can be concentrated into one sitting or distributed across separate sessions. For verbal information that must be recalled later, the reviewed evidence favors genuine spacing over massing, but it does not provide one universal calendar. The useful gap changes with how long the material must remain available.

1. Choose material that fits the evidence boundary

Use this protocol for verbal material you will later need to recall: terms, facts, definitions, paired items, short passages or similar content. Do not treat the same evidence as proof for complex skill transfer, creativity, motor performance or workplace output.

2. State the retention target

Write when you expect to need the material: tomorrow, next week, next month or later. The review found that the spacing associated with the best later recall tended to increase as the retention interval increased, so a schedule cannot be chosen sensibly without this target.

3. Separate the study episodes

Divide the planned repetitions across at least two sessions separated by a real gap. Avoid counting repeated exposure in one uninterrupted sitting as spaced practice. Where possible, keep the planned amount of study comparable so that more total practice is not mistaken for a spacing effect.

4. Use longer gaps for longer retention targets

For material needed over months or years, prefer sessions spread across days or longer rather than completing all review within one day. Do not turn this into a precise universal formula: the review concluded that the exact interval for maximizing long-term retention could not be stated with certainty.

5. Check recall after the intended interval

At the point when the material should be available, test whether you can recall it without relying on the source. Record what was retained and what was lost. This check evaluates the schedule; it does not prove that spacing created application skill or deeper understanding.

6. Adjust the next schedule

If recall was weak, shorten the next gap or add another separated session. If recall was stable, a longer gap may be reasonable. Fixed and expanding schedules were not compared strongly enough in the review to justify one universal algorithm.

Questions to consider

What spacing interval should I use?

There is no universal interval. The review found that the spacing associated with the best later recall tended to increase as the desired retention period increased. For material needed over months or years, use separate sessions across days or longer rather than concentrating everything into one day, then adjust from actual recall.

Should every interval become longer than the previous one?

Not as a rule from this source. Evidence comparing expanding and fixed schedules was limited and inconsistent. Use a schedule you can follow, preserve real gaps between sessions, and do not present an expanding algorithm as universally superior.

Does this apply to every kind of learning?

The reviewed synthesis was restricted to verbal material measured by later recall, including paired associates, lists and related verbal-memory tasks. It does not by itself establish transfer to complex skills, creative work, motor learning, workplace performance or the ability to apply knowledge.

Is studying every day always best?

No. A fixed daily schedule may be convenient, but the useful gap depends on the retention target and material. The review explicitly found that intervals can become too long and that the exact optimum remains uncertain.
