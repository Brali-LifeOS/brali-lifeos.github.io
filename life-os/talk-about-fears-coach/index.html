Talk About a Fear With Someone You Trust

Talk It Out

Protocol summary · reviewed

Try this

Choose someone you trust and tell them, in your own words, what you are worried or afraid about. You can also say what kind of support you want, such as listening, practical help, or simply company. Keep the conversation within boundaries that feel appropriate for both of you.

Check-in: Who did I talk to, what did I want from the conversation, did I feel heard or supported, and is there any next step I need?

Evidence: Reviewed · source · NHS Every Mind Matters — How to talk about your mental health

Talking about fear or anxiety can be difficult, but NHS guidance encourages reaching out to someone you trust and offers practical advice for starting the conversation. Brali treats this as a support-seeking experiment, not a treatment with a promised effect size. The useful question is whether talking helps you feel understood, clarifies what you need, or points toward an appropriate next step.

1. Choose someone you trust

Think of someone who is likely to listen without judging or pressuring you. NHS Every Mind Matters suggests considering who you feel able to talk to. The right person may be different depending on whether you want emotional support, practical help or professional guidance.

2. Start in plain language

You do not need a polished disclosure. A simple sentence such as “I have been worried about something and I would like to talk about it” is enough to begin. Use your own words rather than trying to fit a prescribed script or word count.

3. Say what kind of support you want

If you know what would help, make it explicit. You might want the person to listen, think through one practical next step, or simply spend some time with you. This reduces the pressure on both people to guess what the conversation is supposed to accomplish.

4. Respect both people's boundaries

You can share as much or as little as feels appropriate, and the other person may also have limits on what they can offer. A supportive conversation does not require a fixed timer, a complete life history, or an immediate solution.

5. Notice what the conversation gave you

Afterward, note whether you felt heard, whether anything became clearer, and whether there is a useful next step. Do not expect a required drop in fear or distress. A conversation can be worthwhile even if the fear is still present.

6. Use the right level of support

If fear or anxiety keeps returning, is hard to cope with, or significantly affects daily life, a trusted conversation can be a starting point rather than the whole plan. NHS guidance recommends professional support for anxiety, fear or phobia that is affecting everyday life.

Questions to consider

Who should I talk to?

Choose someone you trust and who is reasonably able to listen. That might be a friend, family member, colleague, GP or mental-health professional depending on the situation. You do not need to disclose everything to one person or share more than feels appropriate.

Do I need a script or a timed conversation?

No. A simple opening can help, but there is no evidence-based word count or required number of minutes. You can start with one honest sentence and tell the other person whether you want listening, practical help, advice, or just some company.

When should I look for professional support?

If anxiety or fear is persistent, difficult to cope with, or affecting everyday life, use appropriate professional support rather than relying only on conversations with friends or family. NHS guidance recommends getting help when phobia or anxiety symptoms are affecting your life.
