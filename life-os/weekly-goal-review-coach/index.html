Run a Weekly Review That Produces One Clear Change

Compare intentions with what actually happened, then update the next week.

Protocol summary · practical

Try this

Review the active goals and commitments, note what moved and what did not, identify the main constraint or changed assumption, decide what should continue, stop, shrink, delegate, or move, and schedule the first concrete action created by the review.

Check-in: What moved this week, what blocked or changed, what am I changing for the next week, and where is the first action scheduled?

The effective public record is a bounded operational review: inspect active commitments, compare intended and actual progress, identify the main changed constraint, make explicit continue/stop/shrink/move/delegate decisions, and schedule the first resulting action.

A weekly review is useful when it changes the next week. The goal is not to produce a perfect retrospective or a large dashboard; it is to compare current commitments with reality, expose the main constraint, and make a small number of explicit decisions about what happens next.

1. Collect the active picture

Open the current goals, task list, calendar, deadlines, and any short notes that explain significant changes. Focus on the commitments that can affect the coming week rather than reopening every historical item.

2. Compare intended and actual progress

For each active goal, note what moved, what stayed blocked, what became irrelevant, and what consumed more or less capacity than expected. Keep facts and assumptions separate when the reason for a miss is still unclear.

3. Make the operating decisions

Decide which items continue unchanged and which should stop, shrink, move, delegate, split, or escalate. Prefer a small number of consequential changes over a long list of cosmetic edits to the plan.

4. Put the first change into the next week

Convert the most important review decision into a visible next action with an owner and an appropriate place in the calendar or task system. A review that ends only with notes has not yet changed the operating plan.

Questions to consider

How long should a weekly review take?

Long enough to make the important decisions and short enough that the review remains maintainable. The right duration depends on the number of active commitments and the quality of the information already captured during the week.

What if the week was disrupted by unexpected work?

Treat the disruption as part of the operating context. Record what changed, which commitments were displaced, and whether the next week needs different capacity, scope, ownership, or escalation rather than judging the original plan in isolation.
