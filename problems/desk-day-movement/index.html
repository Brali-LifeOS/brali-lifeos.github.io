Add movement to a desk-heavy day without a perfect schedule

How can I move more when I sit at a desk most of the day?

Choose repeatable movement opportunities and sustainable exercise without pretending there is one scientifically perfect break interval.

How to use this page: start with the decision path, then choose one protocol that fits the actual bottleneck. Recommendations come only from Brali protocols already classified as reviewed or practical. Decision guidance is editorial synthesis, not a claim that one sequence is universally best.

Choose the bottleneck first

Decision 1

The main problem is uninterrupted sitting

Attach brief movement to transitions you already have: calls, refills, task changes, or natural breaks.

Decision 2

You want a sustainable exercise routine

Choose a small repeatable strength, mobility, or walking commitment that survives ordinary workdays.

Decision 3

You are searching for the perfect cognitive break interval

Use practicality and adherence as the decision criteria. Brali does not treat exploratory cognition findings as a universal dose prescription.

Stop rule: Pain, injury, new severe symptoms, or medical exercise restrictions belong outside a general knowledge library and should be handled with appropriate professional guidance.

Trusted protocols that fit this problem

Flagship 100 · reviewed

Build Toward a Weekly Movement Baseline

Look at the coming week, mark the activity you already do, then add realistic walking, moderate or vigorous activity, and strength sessions where they fit. Build gradually rather than treating the guideline as a one-day target.

Check-in: Does the plan fit your current ability, schedule and any guidance from your health professional? Adjust it rather than forcing a target that is inappropriate for you.

Flagship 100 · reviewed

Start a Morning Stretch Routine

Use a short, gentle flexibility routine as a morning movement cue if that timing suits you. Move slowly only as far as is comfortable, start with a few simple movements, and build up gradually rather than forcing a fixed 10-to-20-minute progression.

Check-in: Did the movements feel comfortable and controlled? Did I stop or modify anything that caused pain or made me feel unwell, and is this routine still appropriate for me?

Flagship 100 · reviewed

Try Short High-Intensity Intervals Safely

If vigorous exercise is appropriate for your current fitness and health, choose a simple movement you can perform safely and try a short interval session at an intensity you can control. Treat 20 seconds of work and 10 seconds of rest as one possible interval format, not a universal prescription, and stop if you feel pain or become unwell.

Check-in: Was this level of intensity appropriate for me today, did I keep good control of the movement, and did I have any pain, dizziness, unusual breathlessness or other reason to stop?

Flagship 100 · reviewed

Use a High-Intensity Interval Timer Safely

If very vigorous exercise is suitable for you, use an interval timer to alternate a short period of harder work with recovery. A 20-second work and 10-second recovery pattern can be one option, but choose a movement and number of rounds you can control safely rather than treating 8 all-out rounds as mandatory.

Check-in: What movement and interval did I use, was the effort appropriate for my fitness today, did I maintain control, and was there any pain or reason to stop?

Evidence boundaries

support-existing · reviewed boundary

Give me a break! A systematic review and meta-analysis on the efficacy of micro-breaks for increasing well-being and performance

Across the included studies, micro-breaks of up to 10 minutes were associated on average with small improvements in vigor and reduced fatigue. The pooled overall performance effect was not statistically significant, and performance effects varied by task demands and break duration. This supports retaining a short, adjustable break as a bounded recovery practice without presenting it as a reliable performance booster.

Does not establish: A 5-minute break is an evidence-based optimal duration. Twenty-five minutes of work followed by five minutes of rest is superior to other focus schedules.

watch · reviewed boundary

Acute cognitive effects of interruptions to prolonged sitting with brief standing or physical activity breaks: a systematic review and three-level meta-analysis

Across the included trials, interruptions to prolonged sitting were associated with small acute improvements in executive function and memory on average. Evidence for global cognition, information processing, and attention was insufficient or inconsistent, and certainty was low for most outcomes.

Does not establish: A specific break frequency is proven to optimize cognition. A specific break duration or intensity should be prescribed for everyone.
